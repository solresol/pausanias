# 1.29.11 — Fortune and the fallen

## Passage and orientation

On the Academy road outside Athens, Pausanias moves from the burial of those who fell near Corinth to Sparta's later defeat at Leuctra and then to an elegiac monument for the dead of several other campaigns. The stone and road anchor his location. The two prose wings carry the sequence from his judgment about Fortune to the verses that remember people lost far from Athens. Leuctra remains a comparison in the text. The art does not conflate it with the graves or the battle near Corinth.

The pictured stones and their placement are imagined. Surviving public infantry and cavalry inscriptions from the Corinthian War support the general use of upright casualty stelai. The design does not identify or reproduce the multi-campaign elegy, presume a specific excavated monument is the one Pausanias saw, or supply any invented wording. ## Three compositions

1. **Selected.** A tall central picture of a public burial marker in context, with separate numbered prose wings and one modest caption below the art. The turn from Corinth and Leuctra to the elegy falls at a natural sentence break. The stone interrupts the two readings and the Academy road gives it a setting. A restrained warm-paper frame separates the art and text. No scenic inset or locator is needed.
2. A wide, shallow procession of stones above an uninterrupted translation. It would show the public setting clearly, but returns to the broad top-image and lower-reading arrangement of 1.29.6. The long view also reduces the prominence of the inscribed surface.
3. A three-panel sequence of the two battle settings and the Athenian monument. It gives the reversals visual space, but shifts the page toward battlefield reconstructions and needs more conjectural details than Pausanias supplies. It also places too much emphasis on Leuctra as a location of commemoration.

The two separate prose wings and central art spine differ from the diagonal diptych and full-bleed maritime inset of the two immediate predecessors. The layout occurs once in the candidate-plus-five window. The left wing carries the first portion of the reading as part of a two-wing arrangement, with neither wing forming a tall full-height translation sidebar.

Compared with 1.29.10, the view changes from distant and low to near and downward, making the cuts and wear of the marble visible. Terracotta, dark olive, ivory and violet shadows replace the sea blues. Clear oblique daylight gives the stone a worked surface, and a small area of funerary architecture takes the place of a whole coastal city. No equipment, battle action, individual portrait, or supernatural figure is needed to convey Pausanias's comparison.

## Art direction

Use one portrait raster in a 1400 × 1780 opening. The most prominent marker stands in the lower middle, with its near edge and base visible and with room around it for cropping. The track and smaller grave architecture recede above and around it. Use material-specific painting, interrupted highlights across the stone and low dry roadside vegetation. The art contains no labels or reserved text areas. The title, Athens orientation, passage ID, numbered headings, exact English and single caption are placed locally.

## Final comparison

The final native PNG and the six preceding thumbnails were reviewed before filing. At thumbnail scale the narrow framed portrait divides two separate text fields, with open paper around both. The immediate predecessors use two offset wide pictures and a full-bleed view around a lower inset. The two-wing layout appears once in the candidate and five predecessors. This page gives the inscribed surface, plinth, and worn edges most of the picture, while the diagonal road and receding markers keep the cemetery legible in depth. The dry terracotta road, warm near stone and patterned sun are distinct from the cool offshore view and broad urban silhouette of 1.29.10. No labels or callouts obscure the illustration.
