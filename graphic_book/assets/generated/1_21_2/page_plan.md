# Page plan — Pausanias 1.21.2

## Passage

`1.21.2`

Pausanias judges the theatre portrait of Aeschylus to be later than both the poet's death and the painting of the battle of Marathon. He then reports that, while still young and keeping watch over grape clusters in a field, Aeschylus fell asleep; Dionysus appeared beside him and commanded him to compose tragedy, which he attempted eagerly at daybreak.

## Key places, events, people, and objects

- The Athenian theatre and its later portrait of Aeschylus
- The painting of the battle of Marathon, used as a chronological marker
- Young Aeschylus asleep while guarding ripe grape clusters
- Fully robed Dionysus standing beside him in the dream
- Dawn and Aeschylus' immediate first attempt to compose tragedy

## Main visual

A richly painted Attic vineyard at the instant before sunrise. Young Aeschylus, fully clothed as a field watchman, has fallen asleep beside baskets and hanging clusters of dark grapes. A dignified, human-scaled, fully robed Dionysus stands beside him in restrained dream light and gestures toward a wax tablet, stylus, and plain theatre mask. The waking horizon and layered vineyard landscape make the sequence from night vision to daybreak legible without generated text.

## Scenic insets

1. **The later portrait:** an inhabited Roman-period Theatre of Dionysus display in which workers or attendants set a fully draped portrait statue of Aeschylus among older poet monuments. The scene makes the statue visibly later than the remembered poet without pretending that its exact form or installation is known.
2. **The Marathon painting:** Athenian viewers before a large painted battle panel representing Marathon, with hoplite ranks and the coastal plain visible within the artwork. The inset concerns the painting and its commemorative function, not a literal reconstruction of the battle itself.

## Orientation strategy

The main visual provides temporal orientation through NIGHT WATCH, DIONYSIAN COMMAND, and DAYBREAK / FIRST TRAGEDY callouts. A short chronology reads `MARATHON PAINTING · AESCHYLUS DIES · LATER PORTRAIT`. A subordinate textured Attica relief locates Athens and Marathon without competing with the scenic art.

## Evidence boundary

Pausanias supplies the relative chronology of portrait, death, and Marathon painting, as well as the youthful field watch, sleep, grape clusters, Dionysus' command, daybreak, eagerness, and first attempt at tragedy. Vineyard location, portrait installation, painting display setting, clothing, tablet, stylus, mask, gestures, and exact visual appearance are reconstructions.
